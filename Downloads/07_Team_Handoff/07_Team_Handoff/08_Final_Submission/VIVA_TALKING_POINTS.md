## Slide 2: Problem and Objectives

**Bryan says:** The business problem is that a consumer lender loses money twice, once when a defaulter is approved and once when a good customer is wrongly rejected. Our goal was to predict payment difficulty at application time so an analyst can prioritise reviews. The hard part is that defaults are only 8 percent of the data, so a lazy model that approves everyone looks 92 percent accurate while catching nothing.

**Thong says:** From the engineering side, our objectives were a clean end-to-end pipeline, a fair comparison of three classifiers under that imbalance, and outputs that are explainable, tested and reproducible. ## Slide 3: Dataset

**Bryan says:** We used the Home Credit Default Risk dataset from Kaggle, real anonymised loan applications released by Home Credit Group. The core table has 307,511 applications and 122 columns, about 166 megabytes, which satisfies the 100,000-record requirement. The chart shows the imbalance, 24,825 defaults which is 8.07 percent.

**Thong says:** Two data quality points we handled at load time. About a quarter of all cells are missing, mostly a sparse building-survey block. **Bryan says:** Three findings shaped the whole project. ## Slide 5: Preprocessing Pipeline

**Thong says:** The flowchart is exactly what src/preprocessing.py implements. We clean, then engineer six affordability ratios such as credit-to-income and annuity-to-income, because a loan that is large relative to income matters more than income alone. We drop the ID, columns that are at least 60 percent missing, and the FLAG_DOCUMENT indicators. The key design decision is the ColumnTransformer, it is fitted on the training fold only, numeric columns get median impute plus standard scaling, categoricals get mode impute plus one-hot with unknown handling. Finally a stratified 80/20 split preserves the 8.07 percent rate in both folds, 246,008 train and 61,503 test, with a fixed random seed.

**Bryan says:** Worth stressing for the panel, because the transformer is inside the pipeline, every statistic it learns comes from training data only, so there is no leakage into the test results you will see in a moment.

## Slide 6: Statistical Evidence

## Slide 7: Models and Imbalance Strategy

**Bryan says:** I built the two reference models. Logistic Regression with balanced class weights is our interpretable baseline, if a simple model were enough we should use it. Random Forest with 200 trees and balanced subsample weighting is the bagging benchmark.

**Thong says:** I built the XGBoost model, 400 trees, depth 6, histogram method, with scale_pos_weight set to the negative-to-positive ratio of the training fold, about 11.4. ## Slide 8: Hold-out Results

**Thong says:** On the 61,503 held-out applications, XGBoost gives the best F1 at 0.290 and the best AUC at 0.763, with recall 0.612, so it catches six in ten defaulters. **Bryan says:** In business terms, RF protects the approval rate but leaks defaults, LR protects against defaults but would drown the review team. XGBoost is the workable middle ground, which is why it is the deployed model behind the dashboard.

## Slide 9: Threshold Tuning

**Thong says:** The default 0.5 cut-off is arbitrary, so as our first optimisation we swept the threshold on the hold-out set. For XGBoost, moving to 0.65 lifts F1 from 0.290 to 0.315, precision improves from 0.190 to 0.260 while recall settles at 0.400. The other two models improve similarly at their own best thresholds.

**Bryan says:** The point we want to land is that the threshold is a business dial, not a maths constant. A lender with a big review team can lower it to catch more defaulters, a lean team raises it for cleaner alerts. We optimised for F1 because we have no real cost matrix, but the sweep makes any other choice a one-line change.

# Q&A Bank: 12 Likely Viva Questions

**1. Why F1 and not accuracy?**
Defaults are 8.07 percent of the data, so a model that predicts "repaid" for everyone scores 92 percent accuracy while catching zero defaulters. Our Random Forest proves the trap, it has the best accuracy, 0.861, yet misses two thirds of defaulters. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall on the default class, so it only rewards models that actually find defaulters without flooding the analysts. We report accuracy and ROC-AUC alongside it for completeness.

**2. Why scale_pos_weight instead of SMOTE?**
We tested it rather than assumed it. xgb_experiment.py ran no correction, weighting only, SMOTE at 0.3 and 0.5 ratios, and combinations, all on the same hold-out. Weighting alone gave the best F1 at the 0.5 threshold. SMOTE creates synthetic minority rows, and combining it with weighting double-corrects, recall rises but precision is crushed. Weighting is also cheaper, it is one parameter, no synthetic data generation, and no risk of synthetic rows leaking across folds.

**3. Why threshold 0.65?**
We swept thresholds on the hold-out set and 0.65 maximises XGBoost's F1, raising it from 0.290 to 0.315 and precision from 0.190 to 0.260. Intuitively, because we weighted the positive class by about 11.4 during training, the model over-scores everyone, so the break-even point sits above 0.5. With a real cost matrix from the business we would pick the threshold that minimises expected loss instead, the sweep makes that a one-line change.

**4. What is the Brier score?**
It is the mean squared error between predicted probability and the actual 0 or 1 outcome, lower is better, and it measures calibration, whether a 0.7 score really means 70 percent risk. Our values are RF 0.124, XGBoost 0.163, LR 0.202, and all calibration curves sit above the diagonal because class weighting inflates the scores. That is why we treat outputs as ranking scores and the dashboard shows percentile bands, not probabilities.

**5. Why cache as parquet?**
The raw CSV is 166 megabytes and pandas must re-parse text and re-infer types on every load, which is slow during iterative development. Parquet is a compressed columnar binary format that stores types, so reloads are several times faster and the file is smaller. The loader writes the cache once and reuses it, which saved us real time across dozens of pipeline runs. For the full 2.7 gigabyte relational set this matters even more.

**6. What is data leakage and how did you prevent it?**
Leakage is when information from the test set influences training, which inflates reported performance. Our main defence is that all preprocessing statistics, imputation medians, modes, scaler parameters and one-hot categories, are learned by a ColumnTransformer fitted on the training fold only, inside the sklearn Pipeline, then merely applied to the test fold. The split itself is stratified with a fixed seed before any fitting. We also dropped the ID column so the model cannot memorise row identity, and the unit tests assert the transformer is fit on train only.

**7. What is the complexity of each model?**
Logistic Regression with liblinear is about O(n times d) per epoch, single-threaded, which is why it is slowest at 95 seconds on our roughly 240-column one-hot matrix. Random Forest is O(T times n log n times square root of d) with T equal to 200 trees, 58.8 seconds. XGBoost with the histogram method is O(T times L times n) after binning features once, and it parallelises, so despite 400 trees it trains fastest at 22.8 seconds. Prediction is around a second for all three, fine for interactive use.

**8. Why drop the FLAG_DOCUMENT_* columns?**
They are around 20 binary flags recording which forms were submitted with the application. They describe the application process rather than the applicant's credit behaviour, most are near-constant so they carry almost no variance, and our screening showed negligible association with the target. Dropping them removes noise and dimensionality from the one-hot matrix, and any apparent signal would be hard to defend because document requirements correlate with product type, which the contract-type feature already captures cleanly.

**9. Gender is predictive. Is it ethical to use it?**
It is predictive, the chi-square is 920.8 and male applicants default at about 10.1 percent versus 7.0, but predictive is not the same as permissible. Equal-credit principles and fairness research, such as Kozodoi and colleagues 2022, treat gender as a protected attribute, and a model using it can discriminate even indirectly. We kept it in the analysis to understand and report the pattern, and we explicitly flag it as an ethical limitation, in production we would remove it and audit for proxies, alongside the GDPR Article 22 requirement that no solely automated decision is made.

**10. How would the other tables help?**
The six child tables hold behavioural history, bureau records of other loans, previous Home Credit applications, monthly POS, instalment and credit-card balances, joined on the application ID. Behaviour usually beats demographics, late instalments or rising card utilisation are direct evidence of payment stress that no application form captures. We would aggregate each child table per applicant, counts, means, recency features, and append them to the feature matrix. Literature on this dataset shows exactly such features driving the winning solutions, which is why it is our top recommendation.

**11. How do your tests prove the code is correct?**
The 20 pytest tests pin down the behaviours the results depend on, the sentinel 365243 becomes NaN, the engineered ratios compute correctly including divide-by-zero cases, the transformer output contains no NaN and is fitted on train only, the stratified split preserves the class rate and row counts, and the optimisation functions return the documented threshold and F1 shapes. The Streamlit AppTest boots the dashboard headlessly and asserts the pages render without exceptions. They are regression armour, any commit that breaks a contract fails the suite before it reaches the trunk.

**12. How does the dashboard map a score to a risk band?**
The Live Prediction page scores the applicant with the trained XGBoost pipeline, then compares that score to the score distribution of the 50,000-row reference sample. Below the 50th percentile, q50, is Low, between q50 and the 80th percentile, q80, is Moderate, above q80 is High. We use percentiles deliberately because the weighted model's raw scores are inflated, the Brier analysis showed they are not calibrated probabilities, so ranking against the population is the honest presentation, and the band thresholds are transparent and easy to retune with the business.
